What everything does

ScaleTune is for instruments whose intonation you have to make yourself — strings, winds, voice. Everything here is a reference to play against, not a thing to listen to.

Modes

Drone
Holds notes indefinitely, to tune against. Several sound together, so a drone can be a chord. The metronome is there too, switched off until you ask for it: a held note, and a pulse to place it against.
Scale
Plays a scale as a line, in the key, range and direction you choose.
Arpeggio
The same, built from a chord instead of a scale.
Free
You pick the notes yourself, each with its own octave, and they play in that order.
Metro
The metronome on its own, with no notes at all. It opens on the tempo and the bar; the count-in, the subdivision, the speed-up and the polyrhythm are under More.
Tuner
Listens instead of sounding. Nothing else plays while it is open — the microphone would hear the app itself.

Tuner

What it reads
The note, how many cents away you are, and both frequencies — the one you are playing and the one you want. Under two cents it turns green.
The strobe
The band of stripes drifts at the difference between the two pitches: still means right, and it drifts the way you are wrong. It shows a quarter of a cent, which no needle can — at A4 that is a tenth of a hertz, one slow crawl every ten seconds. A long way out it blurs, which is what the number above is for.
Reference A4
415 to 466, moving every note with it. 415 for baroque pitch, 442 or 443 where the orchestra sits high.
Temperament
Equal, just, Pythagorean, quarter-comma meantone, Werckmeister III. Each is a set of deviations from equal temperament, counted from the key you name, so the key note is the one everything else is made pure against. Just makes the thirds pure and the fifths nearly so; Pythagorean makes the fifths pure and the thirds wide; meantone buys pure thirds by narrowing every fifth; Werckmeister spreads the error so that every key is playable and none is identical.
What Key does to the reading
In just intonation in C, an A sits about fifteen cents under an equal A — and the tuner will say so. That is not an error to correct; it is what the temperament is. Reference A4 keeps its own job, which is to place the equal grid that the deviations are counted from.
Low strings
It reads down to 27.5 Hz, below the lowest string on a five-string bass. Down there it answers more slowly: a pitch that low needs a fifth of a second of sound before there is anything to measure.

Root

The twelve notes
The key everything else is built from.

Scales and chords

Clear everything
The ✕ beside the ? empties every selection at once — the scale, the chord, the whole study list, the drone's notes, the free notes, the figures and every accent — rather than clearing each panel in turn. It asks first: the second tap within four seconds is the one that does it. It leaves alone what you built rather than chose: the pattern you wrote, the mixer, the tempo and the metronome's settings.
The family boxes
One opens at a time and the others fold away. A closed box still says what is chosen inside it, so folding one never hides a selection.
Symmetric
Three that do not have seven notes. The whole tone has six and no semitone anywhere, so nothing in it points home and any note could be the tonic. The two diminished scales have eight and alternate their steps — whole-half is the classical one, half-whole the one played over a dominant chord. The chromatic has all twelve. None can be spelled with one letter each, so they are written the way players write them rather than the way the rule would force: a rising chromatic in sharps, the diminished without the double flat.
Pentatonic and the blue notes
Five notes, and what is missing is the point: the major has no fourth and no seventh, the minor no second and no sixth, so neither leans anywhere. Blue notes adds one note to each — a flat third over the major, a flat fifth over the minor — which is what turns a pentatonic into a blues scale. It is spelled the way it is written: A blues reads A C D Eb E G, with the flat fifth sharing its letter with the fifth.
Sevenths in one octave
A seventh arpeggio over one octave stops below its top note, the way inversions are practised. Over more than one octave it lands on it.
Single or Study list
The two boxes under the modes. Single works on one scale or chord at a time; Study list collects several and takes them in turn, one per pass, so a whole family can be worked without stopping. It governs every panel below it, which is why it sits above them rather than inside the Root panel where it used to hide.
Each item, its own way
The mark before a name in the rotation is that item's direction, and tapping it goes round up, down, up-and-back, and off again. Off — a faint dot — means it follows Direction like everything else, which is what a rotation usually wants; set it on one item and only that one changes. "The dorian up, then the same arpeggio down" is one exercise in two halves, and this is where that is written. It sits on the chip rather than on the scale's own button because a button that chooses a scale should not also, on some hidden press, choose how it goes.
The study list
One list, holding scales and chords together: pick scales in Scale and chords in Arpeggio and they join the same rotation, so "the dorian, then a minor seventh on the same root" is one exercise rather than two. Each item plays as what it is, whichever panel you happen to be looking at, and the numbers on the buttons count through the whole list. A tap adds, every time, so the same thing can be in twice — a major chord after the ionian and again after the lydian is a real exercise, and a button that only toggled could not say it. A button in the list twice shows both its places. In the rotation itself every item does two things: its ✕ takes it out — four taps by mistake are four taps back — and the item itself picks it up. Picked up, the next one you tap is where it goes; tap the same one again to leave it where it was. Two taps and no dragging, which is the only kind of reordering a finger can do on a phone. Moving while it plays keeps whatever is sounding.
Clear
Empties the selection. With the study list on it drops that panel's kind from the list — the scales from Scale, the chords from Arpeggio — and leaves the rest.

Range & Direction

Octaves
How far the line reaches. If the top would run past the highest sample, the span is reduced until it fits — and grows back by itself once there is room again.
Start Octave
Where the line begins.
Direction
Up, down, or up then back down.
Start On
Which degree of the scale the line starts from — the way a mode is practised from each of its own notes.
Pattern
The shape walked from every degree, rather than straight up. Thirds plays 1–3, then 2–4, then 3–5; 1 2 3 plays 1-2-3, then 2-3-4. Fourths through sevenths are the same idea with a wider reach. The steps are counted in degrees of the scale, not semitones, so thirds are thirds in any mode — and over the chromatic, where a degree is a semitone, the name is the shape rather than the interval. A shape has nowhere to go past the end of the exercise — a third above the top C is outside the octave you asked for — so a pattern always turns there, and it is the shape that inverts. Going up you play C–E, D–F, on to B–D, and the C above turns it over into C–A, a third below: the descent has begun. Coming down the shape keeps reaching under, so D–B passes below the C you set off from before the next C turns it back into C–E. Direction therefore says which end you start at rather than whether it comes back; only a plain scale can run one way and leap back to begin again. The line under the buttons spells out what the one you picked does in the key you are in.

Practice Progressions

Root Movement
Stay in one key, or move up a fourth each time everything else has been round once — the cycle of fourths.
Through the modes
The seven modes of one scale, each from its own note and each one octave: C ionian, then D dorian, then E phrygian, round to B locrian and back to C. The key never changes — the tonic steps up to the next degree and the mode steps with it, which is the same thing said twice — so the whole run is in one key and there is not an accidental in it. Up and Down take every mode the same way. Up-Down and Down-Up make the run itself turn, which is how this is practised: C ionian up to the C above, and the D above that is where D dorian starts, coming all the way down to its own D — from which E is one step up and it turns again. Every handover but one is a single step, and the run stays between the octave you chose and the one above it. The exception is the wrap from the last mode back to the first, where you would take a breath anyway. It wants one seven-note scale chosen on its own: the modes are that scale's own rotations, which a pentatonic, a chord and a symmetric scale do not have. One octave each, because over two the next mode would begin where the ear has lost the thread of the one before — and between them there is a drop back down, which is what your hand does anyway.
What a pass is
One round of the notes. The order is: the rhythmic figure first, then the mode or chord, then the key — so one mode goes through every figure before the next mode starts.

Metronome

Tempo
10 to 300 BPM. Type it, drag it, or tap it in — Tap tempo averages the last few taps.
Bar
How many beats it counts. Tap a circle to cycle it: accented, plain, silent. With notes playing every beat is plain unless you ask otherwise; with the metronome alone the first beat is accented, so the bar you chose can be heard.
Subdivision
A fold, like the pattern and the mixer, saying what it is set to while it is shut. What each beat is divided into. Swing is two ticks split two to one — the triplet feel a written pair of eighths is actually played with. Single uses one figure; Sequence takes the ones you pick in turn, one per pass.
Click
Follow figure clicks every subdivision; Beats only clicks the beats and leaves the figure to the notes.
Count In
Bars of click before the notes come in. It ends on a downbeat: what is left of the bar first, then whole bars, so a phrase that finishes mid-bar does not put your entry off the beat. And the phrase finishes where a phrase finishes: on the handover the line runs to the next bar line and lands there on the tonic of the key it has been in, and only then does the waiting begin — the rest of that bar, and any further bars you asked for. Count again on says when it comes back: on a new key only, which is the moment you always need, or on any change, which includes handing over from one scale or chord to the next in the same key. Never between rounds of the same thing, which would break the very drift the app is built around.
Advance Every
With the metronome alone nothing comes round on its own, so you say what a pass is: so many bars, or so many beats. The figure list and a per-pass tempo change both move on that.
Tempo Change
The tempo moving on its own. Up and Down step it by so many BPM — every pass, or every so many minutes up to thirty. Ramp slides instead: from the tempo you set to the one you name, a little on every beat, over as long as you choose, so there is no moment where it jumps. A ramp starts again from your tempo every time you press Start, because otherwise the second run would have nowhere to go; steps stay where they got to. Either way it stops at 10 and at 300.
Where the accompaniment lives
In Metro, and only there. In the other modes the metronome is something to play against, so it clicks and nothing more — a kit, a mixer and a pattern editor were six controls deep in a panel you open to set a tempo. Choose a style in Metro and it stays chosen; step into Scale and you get the click back.
Accompaniment
Instead of a click, a groove: Rock, Pop, Funk, R&B, Bossa or Jazz Swing, on a kick, a snare, hats, a ride, a wooden clave and a pair of brushes. The Ballad is those brushes: one sweep that never stops, the way a brush is actually played, leaning a little on two and four and breathing on one and three. No cymbal in it unless you ask: Hi-hat on 2 & 4 puts the foot under the sweep, which is the other half of how a ballad is usually played. Try it under 90. Each is a bar of four that repeats — the bossa is two, because its clave is — with no fills and no breaks, so it stays something to play over rather than something to listen to. The swing sits where a swing sits — two thirds through the beat, not half — and its bass drum feathers all four beats so quietly it is felt rather than heard. The styles are written in four, so the bar goes to four while one plays and comes back to what it was when the click returns. The count-in still clicks: there you want a beat and nothing else. The Click slider is its volume. The kit is recorded, not synthesised, and the whole kit was recorded here — bass drum, snare, three hi-hats, ride and clave. The bass drum and the snare come in two takes each, loud and soft, so an accent and a ghost note are different strokes rather than one stroke at two volumes.
Mixer
A level for each drum, over whatever the pattern asks for — the one thing that can answer "the hi-hat is too quiet against the brushes", since what balances on headphones does not balance on a phone. It applies to both kits and to a pattern of your own, and it is kept for next time. A ghost note answers to the snare and the bossa's bass drum to the bass drum, since that is what they are; the faders a style does not use are faded out, so a fader that can do nothing says so. Level again puts every fader back.
Density
How full a style plays. Busy adds what a drummer would add — the extra hits are written into each style, not invented on the fly, because a random note is not a groove. Light takes the ghost notes out and leaves the cymbals on the beat. A pattern of your own is never thinned or filled: it is exactly as full as you made it.
Notes follow
Under a polyrhythm, which of the two layers the notes ride.
Kit
Three. Recorded is a real acoustic kit, warm and big. Mine is a kit recorded here, in this room. Machine is made rather than recorded — dry, tight, and it cuts through a small speaker. Switch between them while something is playing; that is the only way to hear the difference.
Pattern
Write your own. Six voices down the side and a bar across — the bar you chose, so setting Beats to three gives you three to write in, and the pattern loops in three. A written style is still in four and still says so; this one is yours. Loop is how many bars go round: set it to one and the bar you write repeats, set it to four and you have four to fill, with tabs to move between them. Pick a Level — ghost, middling or accented — and every tap writes at it; tapping a cell that already has that level clears it, and tapping one at another level changes it. One tap either way, so a wrong cell costs one tap to undo. Grid is what you are writing on, not what is written: it changes which cells you can put a hit on, and moves nothing that is already there. Sixteenths for straight things, triplets for swung ones, and both can live in the same pattern — straight sixteenths under a swung ride, both playing. A hit written on the grid you are not looking at shows as a dot in the cell it falls inside; tap the dot to remove it, or switch grid to move it. Starting from a style copies that style in and leaves the original untouched, so the styles never get spoiled. With more than one bar there is Copy into this bar, which takes any other bar and puts it here — bar three into bar one as readily as bar one into bar three — for when a bar is a variation of another rather than something new. Up to four bars, for a phrase that takes longer than one. On a phone the bar is stacked two beats to a row, so the cells stay wide enough to hit. All of it can be written while it plays: the pattern, the kit, the density and the levels are read fresh on every beat, so a cell goes in or comes out at the next one and the pulse never moves under your hands. What you write is kept for next time — it is your work, not a setting — and it plays under Accompaniment → Custom.
Polyrhythm
Two pulses at once, any two numbers. The cycle shown is how long until they meet again. A bar and a subdivision mean nothing against two pulses, so they are put away while it is on.

Phrasing

Note Length
Per figure gives a note on every subdivision; Per beat one note a beat; Per bar one note a bar. Under swing the notes take the swing too, long then short.
Phrase Alignment
Let it drift is the exercise: a phrase whose length does not divide the bar walks round against the pulse and only comes back after several rounds, and the metronome is never resynchronised to it. Restart each round puts note one back on beat one every time.

Timbre

Why it matters
You tune by the beating between partials, so a voice with partials is worth more than a clean one. A sine has a single partial: excellent for a unison or an octave, useless for anything else.
Cello and Oboe
Recordings of real players, with the vibrato tracked out — a reference must not wobble — and retuned so every note lands within a cent.
Organ
Synthesised rather than recorded, from the harmonic profile of a church organ. Its pitch is exact by construction, at every note.
Real piano, Piano synth
For the note sequences, where a clear attack makes the rhythm of a line easy to hear. Both fade, so they are not offered as drones.
Vibraphone
A bar is undercut until its modes land on 1 : 4 : 10 — the note, two octaves above it, and a little over three. That ratio is what reads as struck metal rather than as a tone: a piano's partials are a harmonic series and blur together, while these three stand apart. Each fades at its own rate, quickest at the top, so it turns from a ping into a hum. No motor — the fans a vibraphone is named for only move the volume, but a wobble is the last thing an app for hearing intonation needs, and motor off is a normal way to play it.

Settings — the ⚙ in the corner

Note names
What the notes are called on the screen, and nothing else: no pitch moves. A B C is the English convention. Dó Ré Mi is fixed do — the note keeps its name in every key, as it is taught in Portugal, Spain, Italy and France. Cis Des H is the German one, where a sharp is the suffix -is and a flat -es, the seventh letter is H, and the note a semitone under it is called B rather than H flat. The spelling still follows the key: in Db major you get Ré♭ — or Des — never Dó♯.
Theme
Dark or light. It used to be the sun in the header; it lives here now.
